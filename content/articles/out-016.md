# Skinks in California: Species, Identification, Range & Habitat

**California has two native skink species: the Western Skink (*Plestiodon skiltonianus*) and Gilbert's Skink (*Plestiodon gilberti*).** Western Skinks are generally smaller and more strongly striped as adults, while Gilbert's Skinks are larger and usually become more uniformly brown or olive with age. Juvenile tail color helps with identification, but it is not reliable by itself because young Gilbert's Skinks can also show blue.

For the broader U.S. and Canadian regional network, see the [North American skink directory](/wild-skinks/north-america/).

## Which skink species are native to California?

**Two native species are recognized statewide, and both include regional subspecies or populations.**

| Species | Scientific name | Main identification clue | California range pattern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Western Skink** | *Plestiodon skiltonianus* | Smaller; adults usually retain clearer longitudinal striping; juveniles often have bright blue tails | Widespread in suitable habitat across much of California |
| **Gilbert's Skink** | *Plestiodon gilberti* | Larger; adults usually become browner and less distinctly striped; juveniles can have blue, pink, purple, or mixed-color tails | Broadly distributed through interior, foothill, central, and southern parts of the state |

Comparative field references place Western Skinks at up to about **3.4 in (8.6 cm) snout-to-vent length (SVL)** and Gilbert's Skinks at up to about **4.5 in (11.4 cm) SVL**. SVL excludes the tail, which is useful because skinks can lose and regenerate tails.

## How do you tell a Western Skink from a Gilbert's Skink?

**Start with adult pattern and body size, then use juvenile tail color and scale characters when the animal is young or the range overlaps.**

### Adult Western Skink

Typical field characters include:

- a smaller, slimmer body;
- distinct longitudinal striping that often remains visible in adults;
- smooth, glossy scales;
- juveniles with a vivid blue tail;
- usually **7 supralabial scales** along the upper lip;
- usually **4 enlarged nuchal scales** behind the head.

### Adult Gilbert's Skink

Typical field characters include:

- a larger, heavier body;
- adult coloration that is usually brown, olive, or grayish;
- juvenile striping that fades substantially with age;
- breeding males that can develop reddish head coloration;
- usually **8 supralabial scales**;
- usually **3 enlarged nuchal scales**.

These scale counts are useful field characters, but natural variation means they should be combined with geography and overall pattern rather than treated as perfect stand-alone tests.

## What is the blue-tailed skink in California?

**A bright blue-tailed juvenile in California is often a Western Skink, but juvenile Gilbert's Skinks can also have blue tails.**

Young Gilbert's Skinks may show:

- blue;
- pink;
- purple;
- mixed blue-and-pink tail coloration.

A pink or mixed-color tail strongly favors Gilbert's Skink, while a blue tail alone does not identify the species with certainty.

The side pattern can also help. In many Western Skinks, the dark side stripes continue farther onto the tail beyond the hind legs; in many juvenile Gilbert's Skinks, those stripes end closer to the tail base.

For the broader common-name problem, see the [Blue-Tailed Skink Guide](/wild-skinks/blue-tailed-skink/).

## Where in California are skinks found?

**Both species occur across broad parts of the state, but their relative frequency changes with region, elevation, and microhabitat.**

| California region | Western Skink | Gilbert's Skink | Practical identification note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Northern California / Coast Ranges** | Common in suitable woodland, chaparral, rocky, and covered habitats | Less dominant overall than farther south/interior | A blue-tailed juvenile is more likely to be Western, but confirm with pattern and locality |
| **Sierra foothills / interior California** | Present in suitable cooler or sheltered sites | Common in many foothill and interior habitats | This is an important overlap zone, so scale characters become more useful |
| **Central and southern California** | Present in suitable localities | Broadly represented in foothill, woodland, grassland, and mountain settings | Larger brown adults and pink/mixed-tail juveniles often favor Gilbert's |

This is a statewide identification summary, not a substitute for a current county-level range map.

### Are there skinks in Southern California?

**Yes. Gilbert's Skinks are well represented in parts of Southern California, and Western Skinks also occur in suitable local habitats.** Identification should use locality plus morphology rather than assuming every southern California skink is Gilbert's.

## What habitats do California skinks use?

**California skinks depend on accessible shelter, loose or usable ground cover, and nearby thermal refuges rather than one universal moisture level.**

Useful microhabitats include:

- rocks and rock cracks;
- bark and fallen wood;
- leaf litter;
- loose soil beneath cover;
- grass near woodland or shrub cover;
- chaparral edges;
- sun-warmed surfaces close to cooler retreats.

Western Skinks often use relatively moist or sheltered microhabitats, while Gilbert's Skinks can occur in drier inland and foothill settings. Neither species should be reduced to a single statewide habitat formula.

## What do California skinks eat?

**Both native California skinks primarily eat small invertebrates.** Wild prey can include insects, spiders, larvae, and other small arthropods.

The exact prey mix changes with species, body size, habitat, and season. Detailed life history for *P. skiltonianus* belongs in the [Western Skink Guide](/wild-skinks/western-skink/).

## What should you do if you find a skink in your California yard?

**Usually, leave it alone.** A skink using a yard is typically taking advantage of shelter and invertebrate prey.

Helpful coexistence practices include:

- reducing unnecessary pesticide use;
- keeping cats from hunting wildlife;
- leaving stable ground cover in parts of the yard;
- avoiding unnecessary capture or tail handling.

For backyard habitat design, see [Garden Skinks](/backyard-skinks/garden-skinks/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many skink species are native to California?

Two: the Western Skink (*Plestiodon skiltonianus*) and Gilbert's Skink (*Plestiodon gilberti*).

### What is the blue-tailed skink in California?

Often a juvenile Western Skink, but juvenile Gilbert's Skinks can also be blue-tailed. Use locality, body pattern, and scale characters together.

### What is the difference between a Western Skink and Gilbert's Skink?

Western Skinks are generally smaller and retain stronger adult striping. Gilbert's Skinks are larger and usually become more uniformly brown as adults.

### Do Gilbert's Skinks have pink tails?

Some juveniles do. Young Gilbert's Skinks can have pink, blue, purple, or mixed-color tails depending on population and locality.

### Are there skinks in Southern California?

Yes. Gilbert's Skinks are widespread in suitable southern and interior habitats, while Western Skinks also occur in some localities.

### Where do California skinks hide?

Common shelters include rocks, bark, logs, leaf litter, grass near cover, and shallow burrows beneath surface objects.

### Are California skinks poisonous to dogs or cats?

Native California skinks are not considered venomous based on current evidence. Pet ingestion can still involve digestive, parasite, or other non-venom risks. See [Are Skinks Poisonous or Dangerous?](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).
