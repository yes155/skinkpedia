# Skink Supplies Checklist: Enclosure, Heating, Lighting, Substrate & Feeding Gear

**A complete skink setup starts with the species, then the equipment.** The basic supply categories are consistent—secure housing, climate control, lighting where required, substrate, monitoring tools, hides, water, feeding equipment, and cleaning supplies—but the correct size, output, material, and quantity depend on the skink’s adult size and ecology.

This page is the **equipment and buying checklist** for pet skinks. It helps you decide what categories of gear you need and what specifications to verify before purchase. For installation and physical enclosure design, use the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/). For general husbandry principles, use the [skink care guide](/skink-care/).

## Skink supplies checklist at a glance

Use this checklist before bringing the animal home.

| Supply category | Usually needed? | What to verify before buying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Enclosure** | Yes | Adult size, terrestrial vs. arboreal use, ventilation, substrate depth, escape security |
| **Heat source** | Often | Species-specific thermal requirements, enclosure type, room temperature |
| **Thermostatic control** | For controlled heat sources | Compatibility with the heater and the way the heat source operates |
| **Thermometers** | Yes | Accurate measurement at the animal’s usable warm and cool areas |
| **UVB lighting** | Species-dependent | Target exposure, fixture type, distance, barriers, shade |
| **Visible/daylight lighting** | Usually useful | Day/night cycle and enclosure visibility |
| **Hygrometer** | Important for humidity-sensitive species | Measurement range and placement |
| **Substrate** | Yes | Species ecology, moisture needs, burrowing behavior, safety |
| **Hides and cover** | Yes | Animal size, security, placement across usable zones |
| **Water dish** | Yes | Stability, accessibility, easy cleaning |
| **Feeding equipment** | Species-dependent | Diet type and food presentation |
| **Supplements** | Species-dependent | Diet, age, UV exposure, species-specific guidance |
| **Cleaning supplies** | Yes | Reptile-safe use, easy rinsing, no harmful residues |
| **Scale** | Useful | Safe weight monitoring for the size of the animal |
| **Transport container** | Useful | Secure, ventilated, appropriately sized |

Do not buy every item marketed for “reptiles.” Buy only what solves a documented husbandry need for the species you are keeping.

## 1. Enclosure

**The enclosure is the first major purchase because every other piece of equipment has to function inside it.**

Choose housing based on:

- expected adult size;
- terrestrial, fossorial, arboreal, or other habitat use;
- required substrate depth;
- ventilation;
- heat and humidity retention;
- lighting installation;
- access for cleaning;
- escape resistance.

Common enclosure materials include PVC/composite, sealed wood, and glass. None is automatically best for every skink.

### What to check before buying

Ask:

- Will the adult skink have enough usable space?
- Can the enclosure hold the required substrate depth?
- Can heat and lighting be installed safely?
- Is there enough distance to create species-appropriate environmental zones?
- Are doors, cable ports, and vents escape-resistant?
- Can the enclosure tolerate the expected moisture level?

Exact enclosure dimensions belong to species-specific care pages. For example, the RSPCA source in the cited source material gives **120 × 60 × 60 cm** as a minimum for a single blue-tongue skink. That is a **blue-tongue value**, not a universal skink enclosure size.

For assembly, placement, DIY construction, and climate-zone design, use the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## 2. Heating equipment

**A heater should be selected to achieve the species’ documented temperature pattern safely, not by copying a wattage from another enclosure.**

Possible equipment categories include:

- overhead basking lamps;
- ceramic heat emitters in appropriate applications;
- other reptile-safe heat sources supported by the species’ husbandry plan.

The correct product depends on enclosure size, enclosure material, room temperature, mounting distance, and the temperatures the species needs.

### Do not shop by wattage alone

A “75 W” or “100 W” bulb is not itself a husbandry specification. The same bulb can produce very different temperatures in two different enclosures.

Buy heating equipment only after you know:

1. the target temperatures for the species;
2. the distance from heater to usable surface;
3. the enclosure material and ventilation;
4. how the heat source will be controlled;
5. how temperatures will be measured independently.

## 3. Thermostat or heat controller

**A compatible thermostat or heat-control device is one of the most important safety purchases in a heated reptile enclosure.**

The controller should match the type of heater it regulates. For example, a light-producing basking lamp may require a different control strategy from a non-light-emitting heater.

When comparing controllers, look for:

- compatibility with the heat source;
- sufficient power rating for the equipment;
- an appropriately placed temperature probe;
- clear adjustment and display;
- reliable operation after power interruptions;
- product instructions suitable for reptile use.

A thermostat controls the heater; it does not replace independent thermometers used to verify what the animal actually experiences.

## 4. Thermometers

**Use thermometers to verify the enclosure environment rather than judging temperature by touch.**

A practical setup may use:

- digital probe thermometers;
- multiple digital sensors;
- an infrared thermometer for surface checks where appropriate.

For a species that uses a warm and cool area, measure both. Place sensors at relevant animal height and in locations that reflect actual usable zones.

An infrared thermometer can be valuable for surface temperatures, but it does not replace an air-temperature probe when air temperature is the value you need.

## 5. UVB lighting

**UVB equipment should be purchased only after the species’ target exposure is known.** Do not choose a lamp simply because its package says it is suitable for reptiles.

For species requiring UVB, compare:

- fixture type;
- lamp output;
- enclosure height;
- distance from lamp to the animal;
- mesh or other barriers;
- size of the illuminated zone;
- availability of shade;
- replacement guidance.

Linear UVB fixtures are commonly used because they can create a broader exposure zone than small compact lamps, but the correct output still depends on the species and installation.

The RSPCA blue-tongue guide specifies a **UVI gradient of about 3.0–5.0 at the basking area, grading to zero in shade**. This is a species-specific example, not a universal target for all skinks.

For installation and placement, use the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## 6. Visible lighting and timers

**A stable day/night cycle is part of enclosure management even when the heat source does not provide useful visible light.**

Useful equipment can include:

- visible daylight fixtures;
- programmable timers;
- smart or mechanical timers that maintain a consistent schedule.

The exact photoperiod should follow species-specific guidance rather than a universal 12-hour rule.

Timers are useful because they reduce day-to-day inconsistency, but they should not automate a setup that has never been measured and tested manually.

## 7. Humidity monitoring

**Humidity-sensitive skinks benefit from reliable digital monitoring rather than guesswork.**

A digital hygrometer can help evaluate:

- ambient relative humidity;
- changes after misting;
- ventilation effects;
- seasonal room changes;
- whether substrate moisture is influencing the enclosure as intended.

Place the sensor where its reading is relevant to the animal rather than automatically at the top of the enclosure.

A hygrometer is especially important for forest or moisture-dependent species. Dry-adapted species still need hydration, but they may require very different ambient humidity.

## 8. Substrate

**Substrate should be chosen by species ecology and enclosure function, not by one universal recipe.**

Possible substrate categories include:

- soil-type mixes;
- bark-based substrates;
- coconut-based materials;
- leaf litter;
- sand or sand-dominant systems for appropriate species;
- other specialist substrates documented for the species.

The substrate may need to support:

- burrowing;
- humidity retention;
- drainage;
- surface traction;
- natural foraging behavior.

### How much substrate should you buy?

Buy enough to create the depth required by the species and enclosure design.

Do not use a universal “4–6 inch” rule. A burrowing skink may need meaningful depth, while another species may use substrate differently.

Before purchasing, calculate:

**enclosure floor area × planned substrate depth**

That gives a better estimate of how much material is required than buying bags at random.

## 9. Hides and visual cover

**A hide is functional equipment, not decoration.** It should let the skink shelter securely without trapping it or consuming all usable enclosure space.

Useful categories include:

- commercial reptile hides;
- cork bark;
- stable naturalistic shelters;
- hollow logs or equivalent structures;
- species-appropriate elevated shelter for arboreal skinks.

Where the species uses a thermal gradient, provide secure access to more than one usable environmental zone so the animal does not have to choose between hiding and thermoregulation.

For humidity-dependent species, a moist retreat may be part of the species-specific plan, but it should not be treated as mandatory for every skink.

## 10. Climbing and structural furnishings

**Branches, ledges, logs, rocks, and cork should match the way the species naturally moves.**

Before buying a furnishing, ask:

- Is the species likely to use it?
- Can it support the skink’s full weight?
- Can it shift if the animal digs underneath?
- Is the surface free from dangerous sharp edges?
- Can it be cleaned?
- Does it block heat, UVB, or ventilation?

Arboreal skinks need meaningful elevated routes. Terrestrial skinks may benefit more from low stable obstacles and cover than from tall decorative branches.

For behavioral use of enclosure structure, see the [skink exercise and enrichment guide](/skink-care/exercise-enrichment/).

## 11. Water dish

**A skink water dish should be stable, accessible, and easy to clean.**

Choose a dish based on:

- animal size;
- likelihood of tipping;
- enclosure humidity;
- substrate contamination;
- whether the species uses the dish only for drinking or also enters it.

A large water bowl can affect humidity, so placement should be part of the overall enclosure plan rather than an afterthought.

Clean and refill the dish whenever it becomes soiled.

## 12. Feeding gear

**Feeding equipment depends on whether the skink is insectivorous, omnivorous, or herbivorous.**

Potential supplies include:

- washable food dishes;
- feeding tongs where useful;
- containers for feeder insects;
- feeder-insect food for gut-loading;
- food-preparation tools;
- storage containers for supplements;
- a dedicated cutting surface for plant foods.

Do not let feeding tools dictate the diet. Identify the species’ nutritional needs first.

For food categories, feeding principles, and supplementation boundaries, use [what skinks eat](/skink-care/diet/).

## 13. Calcium and vitamin supplements

**Supplements are species- and diet-dependent supplies, not universal items to use on the same schedule for every skink.**

Before purchasing calcium or a multivitamin, check:

- whether supplementation is recommended for the species;
- the animal’s age;
- whether UVB is used;
- whether the product contains vitamin D3;
- the nutritional profile of the staple foods;
- species-specific frequency guidance.

Avoid buying multiple overlapping vitamin products without a clear nutritional reason.

## 14. Cleaning and maintenance supplies

**Cleaning supplies should help maintain hygiene without leaving harmful residues in the enclosure.**

Useful categories include:

- dedicated waste-removal tools;
- washable buckets or containers;
- disposable paper products where needed;
- reptile-safe cleaning products used according to label directions;
- brushes or sponges reserved for animal equipment;
- gloves for cleaning waste where appropriate.

Do not use strongly scented household products, essential oils, or unverified disinfectant mixtures around the animal.

Cleaning frequency depends on substrate, humidity, enclosure size, species, and actual soiling rather than one fixed calendar.

## 15. Weight-monitoring equipment

**A digital scale is a useful monitoring tool because trends in body weight can help identify growth or health changes.**

Choose a scale that:

- has an appropriate capacity;
- gives useful resolution for the animal’s size;
- can hold a secure container or platform;
- can be cleaned.

For a small skink, a gram scale may be more informative than a large household scale.

Weight is only one health measure. Interpret it alongside body condition, appetite, behavior, and species-specific growth expectations.

See the [skink lifespan, size and growth guide](/pet-skinks/lifespan-size-growth/) for growth tracking.

## 16. Transport and temporary containment

**A secure temporary container is useful for veterinary visits, enclosure maintenance, and transport.**

It should be:

- escape-resistant;
- ventilated;
- appropriately sized;
- easy to clean;
- free of loose heavy objects during transport.

Temporary containers are not substitutes for the permanent enclosure.

For purchase-day planning, see the [skink cost and buying guide](/pet-skinks/cost-buying-guide/).

## 17. Bioactive skink setup supplies

**A bioactive enclosure is optional, not automatically superior to a well-maintained conventional setup.**

A bioactive system may add:

- drainage components where required;
- appropriate soil or substrate layers;
- leaf litter;
- live plants;
- cleanup organisms;
- lighting suitable for both the skink and plants.

The design still has to meet the skink’s temperature, humidity, substrate, space, and hygiene needs.

Do not choose a bioactive setup because it is marketed as “self-cleaning.” Waste removal, environmental monitoring, animal observation, and maintenance are still required.

For the physical design of a bioactive or DIY habitat, use the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## 18. DIY skink enclosure shopping list

**A DIY enclosure can reduce or redistribute costs, but it adds responsibility for materials, ventilation, sealing, electrical safety, and escape prevention.**

Possible build categories include:

- enclosure panels or sealed structural material;
- safe fasteners;
- tracks, hinges, or doors;
- ventilation components;
- sealant appropriate for the intended environment;
- cable-routing hardware;
- lamp guards and mounting hardware;
- substrate barrier or lip;
- locks where needed.

Do not use the supplies page as a construction blueprint. Exact build methods, curing, mounting, and electrical layout belong in the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## What to buy before the skink arrives

**The enclosure and all life-support equipment should be purchased, installed, tested, and stable before the animal comes home.**

Buy and test first:

- enclosure;
- heating;
- thermostat/control;
- temperature monitoring;
- UVB and lighting where required;
- humidity monitoring where relevant;
- substrate;
- hides;
- water dish;
- primary feeding equipment;
- initial food and required supplements.

Useful items such as decorative enrichment can be added later if the core habitat already works.

## What not to buy yet

Avoid purchasing these until species requirements are confirmed:

- a fixed enclosure size based only on the word “skink”;
- a specific basking-bulb wattage;
- a UVB percentage or UVI target copied from another species;
- a generic “tropical reptile” substrate;
- vitamin products without a species-specific supplementation plan;
- tall climbing décor for a ground-dwelling animal;
- automated misting for a species that does not require it;
- branded accessories that solve no defined husbandry need.

This is one of the easiest ways to reduce wasted spending.

## Prioritize products by husbandry consequence

If the budget is limited, prioritize equipment that directly controls welfare before decorative upgrades.

### Priority 1: life-support equipment

- appropriate enclosure;
- safe heating and control;
- reliable thermometers;
- UVB where required;
- humidity monitoring where required;
- substrate;
- shelter;
- water access.

### Priority 2: feeding and maintenance

- food dishes and feeder equipment;
- supplements where required;
- scale;
- cleaning tools.

### Priority 3: optional upgrades

- decorative backgrounds;
- premium enclosure furniture;
- automation that does not replace monitoring;
- nonessential visual accessories.

A product is worth recommending when it solves a clear husbandry problem, not simply because it offers an affiliate opportunity.

## Species-specific supply planning

The final shopping list should change with the species.

For example:

- a [blue-tongue skink](/pet-skinks/blue-tongue-skink/) needs equipment sized for a large terrestrial animal;
- a [red-eyed crocodile skink](/pet-skinks/red-eyed-crocodile-skink/) needs supplies appropriate to a humid, cover-oriented forest-floor setup;
- a [fire skink](/pet-skinks/fire-skink/) requires equipment that supports terrestrial burrowing behavior;
- an [emerald tree skink](/pet-skinks/emerald-tree-skink/) needs usable vertical structure;
- a [sandfish skink](/pet-skinks/sandfish-skink/) requires a setup designed around fossorial movement in dry substrate.

This is why the checklist organizes **product classes** while species pages supply the exact values.

## Frequently asked questions
### What supplies do I need for a pet skink?

At minimum, plan for a species-appropriate enclosure, climate control, temperature monitoring, substrate, hides, water, feeding equipment, and cleaning supplies. UVB, humidity monitoring, supplements, and other gear depend on the species.

### What should I buy first for a skink?

Choose the species first, then buy the enclosure and life-support equipment. Install and test the complete habitat before buying or collecting the animal.

### Do all skinks need UVB lights?

UVB recommendations vary by species. Where evidence supports UVB, choose equipment capable of producing the species-specific exposure while preserving shade.

### Do I need a thermostat for a skink enclosure?

A compatible heat-control device is strongly important when using a controllable heat source. The exact controller should match the heater and enclosure design.

### Is a bioactive skink setup necessary?

No. Bioactive husbandry is optional. A conventional enclosure can work well when it provides the correct environment, hygiene, shelter, and behavioral opportunities.

### Can I build a DIY skink enclosure?

Yes, if the finished enclosure safely meets the species’ requirements for space, climate, ventilation, security, substrate, lighting, and cleaning. Use the [enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/) for implementation.
