# Blue-Tailed Skink Guide: Identification, Species, Diet, Size & Range

**“Blue-tailed skink” is a descriptive common name, not one single species.** In North America, the name is often used for juveniles of several *Plestiodon* species that have a vivid blue tail, while in the Indo-Pacific it can also refer to species such as *Emoia caeruleocauda*. Correct identification therefore starts with **location → body pattern → scale characters → life stage**, not tail color alone.

This guide focuses on **common-name disambiguation**. Dedicated species details belong on pages such as the [five-lined skink guide](/wild-skinks/five-lined-skink/), [broad-headed skink guide](/wild-skinks/broad-headed-skink/), and [western skink guide](/wild-skinks/western-skink/).

## What is a blue-tailed skink?

**A blue-tailed skink is usually a skink with conspicuous blue tail coloration, especially during the juvenile stage.** The name can refer to different species depending on where the animal is found.

Common examples in the available research include:

| Species | Where the “blue-tailed” label applies | Identification context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Common Five-lined Skink (*Plestiodon fasciatus*)** | Eastern North America | Juveniles have five pale stripes and a bright blue tail |
| **Southeastern Five-lined Skink (*Plestiodon inexpectatus*)** | Southeastern United States | Similar juvenile pattern; scale characters help separate it from *P. fasciatus* |
| **Broad-headed Skink (*Plestiodon laticeps*)** | Southeastern United States | Juveniles are striped and blue-tailed; adult males become much larger and develop broad orange-red heads |
| **Western Skink (*Plestiodon skiltonianus*)** | Western North America | Juveniles have a vivid blue tail and a striped dark body |
| **Blue-tailed Copper-tailed Skink (*Emoia caeruleocauda*)** | Indo-Pacific | A different genus; blue tail coloration is not simply the juvenile form of a North American *Plestiodon* |

Because several species share this appearance, “blue-tailed skink” should not be treated as a scientific name.

## How do you identify a blue-tailed skink?

**Start with geography, then compare body stripes, scale patterns, body size, and age.**

### Eastern North America

A small dark skink with five pale stripes and a blue tail is often a juvenile *Plestiodon*. The main lookalikes include:

- Common Five-lined Skink;
- Southeastern Five-lined Skink;
- Broad-headed Skink.

These species can be difficult to separate from photographs alone, especially as juveniles.

### Western North America

A blue-tailed juvenile in the western United States or southern British Columbia may be a Western Skink (*Plestiodon skiltonianus*).

### Indo-Pacific islands

A blue-tailed skink in Oceania or parts of Southeast Asia may belong to *Emoia* rather than *Plestiodon*.

For a broader regional overview, use [North American skinks by state and region](/wild-skinks/north-america/).

## Why do juvenile skinks have blue tails?

**The bright tail appears to function as a visual decoy that can redirect a predator’s attention away from the head and body.**

Juvenile skinks may also move or flick the tail during escape. If a predator grabs it, many skinks can shed part of the tail through **caudal autotomy**.

The detached tail can continue moving for a period after separation, which may help occupy the predator while the skink escapes.

It is better to describe the blue tail as **conspicuous anti-predator coloration** rather than calling it “aposematic” by default. Aposematism specifically refers to warning coloration associated with defense or unpalatability, while the evidence here is centered on distraction and attack deflection.

For the anatomy and regrowth process, see [why skinks drop their tails](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/).

## Does the blue tail disappear with age?

**In several North American *Plestiodon* species, the blue juvenile tail becomes duller as the skink matures.**

The exact adult coloration differs by species and sex. For example:

- adult female five-lined skinks may retain more of the juvenile stripe pattern;
- adult males can lose much of the juvenile striping;
- male Broad-headed Skinks can develop a conspicuous orange-red head.

This is one reason juvenile and adult skinks of the same species can look surprisingly different.

## How big are blue-tailed skinks?

**There is no single adult size because “blue-tailed skink” covers multiple species.**

Examples from the published evidence include:

- Western Skink: approximately **6–8 inches** total length;
- Common Five-lined Skink: generally smaller than the largest Broad-headed Skinks;
- Broad-headed Skink: up to about **8–13 inches** total length.

When identifying an animal, size should be used together with location and morphology rather than alone.

## What do blue-tailed skinks eat?

**Most of the North American species commonly called blue-tailed skinks are insectivorous or invertebrate-focused predators.**

Typical wild prey can include:

- spiders;
- beetles;
- crickets and grasshoppers;
- insect larvae;
- termites;
- small worms and other invertebrates.

Because the common name spans multiple species, do not publish one exact feeding list as if every “blue-tailed skink” has an identical diet.

## Do blue-tailed skinks lose their tails?

**Many North American blue-tailed *Plestiodon* skinks can shed the tail as a defense.**

Tail loss has costs. Regrowth requires energy, and the replacement tail is not identical to the original structure. A dropped tail should therefore be treated as an emergency defense, not a harmless routine behavior.

## Are blue-tailed skinks poisonous?

**The North American *Plestiodon* species discussed on this page are not known as venomous skinks.** Broader questions about myths involving dogs, cats, bites, and toxicity belong on the dedicated [skink safety guide](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

Avoid using “completely harmless” as a universal phrase: any wild animal can bite defensively, and pet-safety questions can involve more than venom or poison.

## Can you keep a blue-tailed skink as a pet?

**Do not assume that a wild blue-tailed skink should be collected for captivity.** Wildlife collection laws vary by species and location, and wild animals can be difficult to establish in captivity.

If the goal is to keep a skink, it is generally better to choose a legally available captive-bred species with established husbandry information. See the [pet skink species guide](/pet-skinks/).

## Frequently asked questions
### Is a blue-tailed skink the same as a five-lined skink?

Not always. Juvenile Common Five-lined Skinks are often called blue-tailed skinks, but the name can also refer to Southeastern Five-lined, Broad-headed, Western, and some Indo-Pacific skinks.

### Why is a skink’s tail blue?

In juvenile *Plestiodon*, the conspicuous tail appears to help redirect predator attention away from the body and toward a detachable part of the animal.

### Do blue-tailed skink tails grow back?

Many skinks can regenerate a lost tail, although the replacement is structurally different from the original. See the [tail autotomy guide](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/) for the full explanation.

### Are blue-tailed skinks poisonous to dogs or cats?

The North American species discussed here are not treated as venomous skinks. Pet-ingestion and toxicity questions are covered in the [skink poison and pet-safety guide](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

### How large does a blue-tailed skink get?

It depends on the species. Western Skinks are around 6–8 inches, while Broad-headed Skinks can reach roughly 8–13 inches.

### How much does a blue-tailed skink cost?

There is no meaningful single price because “blue-tailed skink” is not one pet species. Price depends on the exact species, legal availability, captive-bred supply, and seller.
