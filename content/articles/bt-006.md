# Blue-Tongue Skink Types: Northern, Indonesian, Eastern & Other *Tiliqua*

**The genus *Tiliqua* currently contains seven recognized species, but common pet-trade names also refer to subspecies and locality forms within those species.** This distinction matters because a Northern blue-tongue, an Eastern blue-tongue, an Indonesian blue-tongue, a Merauke, and a Halmahera are not all equivalent taxonomic units.

This guide organizes blue-tongue diversity as **species/subspecies → locality → appearance → husbandry differences**. Detailed care remains on the [blue-tongue skink care guide](/pet-skinks/blue-tongue-skink/care/).

## The seven recognized *Tiliqua* species

Current Reptile Database taxonomy recognizes:

| Species | Common context |
| --- | --- |
| ***Tiliqua adelaidensis*** | Pygmy blue-tongue |
| ***Tiliqua gigas*** | Indonesian / giant blue-tongue complex |
| ***Tiliqua multifasciata*** | Centralian blue-tongue |
| ***Tiliqua nigrolutea*** | Blotched blue-tongue |
| ***Tiliqua occipitalis*** | Western blue-tongue |
| ***Tiliqua rugosa*** | Shingleback / stumpytail |
| ***Tiliqua scincoides*** | Common blue-tongue, including Eastern and Northern subspecies |

This is why “Northern” and “Eastern” should not be presented as separate species.

## Northern blue-tongue skink

**The Northern blue-tongue skink is *Tiliqua scincoides intermedia*, a subspecies of *Tiliqua scincoides*.** It occurs in northern Australia and is one of the best-known captive blue-tongues.

Northern blue-tongues are large, terrestrial skinks and can fall within the 50–60 cm total-length range described in general blue-tongue care references.

Avoid calling the Northern “the largest” or “the most popular” unless you have a current source directly supporting that comparison.

## Eastern blue-tongue skink

**The Eastern blue-tongue is *Tiliqua scincoides scincoides*, the nominate subspecies of *T. scincoides*.** It occurs in eastern and southeastern Australia.

The comparative biomechanical dataset reports approximately **262 mm average SVL** and **158.49 N average bite force** for *Tiliqua scincoides*. Those figures describe the dataset used in the cited sources and should not be treated as a guaranteed value for every Eastern individual.

Temperament should also be described at the individual level rather than assuming all Easterns are docile.

## Tanimbar blue-tongue skink

**The Reptile Database recognizes *Tiliqua scincoides chimaerea* as a third subspecies of *Tiliqua scincoides*, associated with the Tanimbar and Babar Islands of Indonesia.**

This is taxonomically separate from the *Tiliqua gigas* complex and is useful to include because pet-trade discussions can blur Indonesian blue-tongue labels.

## Indonesian blue-tongue skink complex

**The species *Tiliqua gigas* contains three recognized subspecies: *T. g. gigas*, *T. g. evanescens*, and *T. g. keyensis*.**

The Reptile Database lists *T. gigas* across parts of Indonesia and Papua New Guinea.

### Merauke

The name **Merauke blue-tongue** is generally associated with *Tiliqua gigas evanescens*. The subspecies occurs in parts of southern/eastern New Guinea and Indonesian Papua.

### Kei Island blue-tongue

***Tiliqua gigas keyensis*** is associated with the Kei and nearby island region.

### Halmahera

“Halmahera” is a locality/trade label associated with *Tiliqua gigas* populations from Halmahera rather than a separate recognized species.

### Irian Jaya

“Irian Jaya blue-tongue” is widely used in the pet trade, but it should not automatically be presented as a formal species or subspecies name. Use the seller’s locality claim cautiously unless the animal’s taxonomic identification is documented.

## Husbandry differences among blue-tongue types

**Correct identification matters because humidity and other environmental needs vary across *Tiliqua*.** The RSPCA gives a broad blue-tongue humidity range of approximately **40–90% depending on species/locality**, rather than supporting one narrow genus-wide target.

This means a types page should explain that:

- Australian and Indonesian forms can differ in climate exposure;
- locality identification matters;
- exact humidity values should be taken from a taxon-specific source;
- a generic “Northern 40–50 / Indonesian 60–80” rule should not be published unless each figure is directly supported.

For the general care framework, see [blue-tongue skink care](/pet-skinks/blue-tongue-skink/care/).

## Shingleback skink

**The Shingleback is *Tiliqua rugosa*, a distinct species in the genus.** It is known for a short, thick tail and heavy scales and occurs across parts of Australia.

Long-term pair fidelity has been documented in Shinglebacks, making their social behavior especially notable. Detailed pair-bond biology belongs on the dedicated [Shingleback skink guide](/pet-skinks/shingleback-skink/) rather than being expanded here.

Do not describe the species as globally “endangered” without a current conservation source. Legal protection and collection rules also vary by jurisdiction.

## Centralian, Western, Blotched and Pygmy blue-tongues

The genus also includes:

- ***Tiliqua multifasciata*** — Centralian blue-tongue;
- ***Tiliqua occipitalis*** — Western blue-tongue;
- ***Tiliqua nigrolutea*** — Blotched blue-tongue;
- ***Tiliqua adelaidensis*** — Pygmy blue-tongue.

These species differ in range, body form, ecology, and conservation context. They should not be forced into the same captive-care description as the commonly kept Northern or Indonesian forms.

## Blue-tongue color morphs

**Captive breeding has produced blue-tongue skinks sold under color and pattern morph labels such as axanthic, melanistic, albino, caramel, and other breeder-line names.** Morph terminology can describe phenotype, genetics, or breeder lineage, so the inheritance of a specific morph should be confirmed rather than assumed from appearance alone.

Morph does not replace taxonomic identification. A buyer still needs to know:

- species;
- subspecies/locality;
- parentage where available;
- breeder line;
- documented genetic status if the morph claim is important.

Morph pricing belongs in the [blue-tongue skink cost and buying guide](/pet-skinks/blue-tongue-skink/cost-buying/).

## Quick identification hierarchy

When identifying a blue-tongue skink, use this order:

1. **Genus:** Is it a *Tiliqua*?
2. **Species:** Which of the seven recognized species?
3. **Subspecies:** Does the species have a recognized subspecies?
4. **Locality:** Is the label geographic rather than taxonomic?
5. **Morph:** Is the color label genetic, visual, or a breeder-line term?

This prevents pet-trade names from being mistaken for formal taxonomy.

## Frequently asked questions
### How many species of blue-tongue skink are there?

The Reptile Database currently recognizes seven species in the genus *Tiliqua*.

### Is a Northern blue-tongue a separate species?

No. Northern blue-tongues are *Tiliqua scincoides intermedia*, a subspecies of *Tiliqua scincoides*.

### Is a Merauke blue-tongue a species?

Merauke is generally associated with *Tiliqua gigas evanescens*, a subspecies of *Tiliqua gigas*.

### Is Halmahera a separate species?

No. “Halmahera” is a locality/trade label associated with *Tiliqua gigas* populations rather than a currently recognized separate species.

### Do all blue-tongue types need the same humidity?

No. The RSPCA specifically notes that humidity depends on the exact species/locality and gives a broad 40–90% range across blue-tongue skinks.
