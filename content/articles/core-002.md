# Pet Skinks: Best Species, Temperament, Cost & Ownership Guide

A **pet skink** can be a good fit for the right keeper, but suitability varies sharply by species. Blue-tongue skinks, fire skinks, crocodile skinks, tree skinks, sandfish skinks, and prehensile-tailed skinks differ in enclosure style, diet, activity, handling tolerance, and long-term care.

If you are still learning what skinks are as a family, start with our [general skink guide](/skinks/). If you already know you want a captive skink, use this page to compare species and narrow your choice before moving into species-specific care.

## Do skinks make good pets?

**Some skink species make very suitable captive reptiles, but there is no single “skink care” profile that fits the whole family.** A good match depends on the animal’s adult size, habitat use, diet, activity pattern, handling tolerance, availability of captive-bred stock, and the keeper’s ability to maintain the required environment.

Before choosing a species, make sure you are comfortable with:

- providing a secure enclosure large enough for the adult animal;
- maintaining the species-appropriate temperature, lighting, and humidity conditions;
- sourcing the correct foods and supplements;
- paying for ongoing electricity, substrate, food, replacement equipment, and veterinary care;
- keeping the animal for its full lifespan rather than treating it as a short-term pet.

Once you have chosen a species, the [general skink care guide](/skink-care/) explains the husbandry variables that every keeper should understand before building a setup.

## Best pet skink species to compare

The “best” pet skink depends on what you want from the animal. Some are terrestrial and relatively easy to observe, some spend much of their time buried, some are arboreal, and some are better suited to keepers who prefer observation over regular handling.

| Species | Typical captive lifestyle | Diet category | Handling/interaction fit | Best suited to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Blue-tongue skink** | Large terrestrial skink | Omnivorous | Often selected by keepers who want a larger, more visible skink; individual temperament varies | Keepers with room for a substantial terrestrial setup |
| **Fire skink** | Terrestrial and burrowing | Insectivorous | More observation-oriented because it commonly uses deep cover and substrate | Keepers who enjoy natural burrowing behavior |
| **Red-eyed crocodile skink** | Forest-floor, cover-oriented | Insectivorous | Better treated as an observation species than a frequently handled pet | Keepers comfortable managing a humid, low-disturbance setup |
| **Emerald tree skink** | Arboreal and active | Primarily insectivorous | Better for keepers who value climbing activity and visual observation | Keepers able to provide vertical space and structure |
| **Monkey-tailed / prehensile-tailed skink** | Arboreal | Herbivorous | Large, specialized species with more demanding space and husbandry needs | Experienced keepers prepared for a large tropical enclosure |
| **Sandfish skink** | Fossorial desert skink | Predominantly animal-based/invertebrate diet | Primarily an observation animal because much of its activity occurs beneath substrate | Keepers interested in desert and burrowing behavior |
| **Schneider’s skink** | Terrestrial, arid-adapted | Omnivorous/invertebrate-heavy | Often visible and active when established | Keepers interested in an arid terrestrial species |

## Which pet skink is best for a beginner?

**Blue-tongue skinks are often the first species considered by beginners because they are large, terrestrial, widely documented in captive-care sources, and generally easier to observe than secretive or highly specialized species.** That does not make them low-maintenance: they still require substantial space, controlled heating and lighting, an appropriate diet, and long-term care.

A fire skink may suit a beginner who is comfortable with a more secretive animal and does not expect frequent handling. By contrast, red-eyed crocodile skinks and large arboreal species such as prehensile-tailed skinks place more emphasis on specialized habitat management and are usually better choices after a keeper has experience maintaining reptile environments.

The right beginner species is therefore the one whose **adult enclosure, diet, environmental requirements, and behavior you can realistically support**, not simply the species with the calmest reputation.

## Pet skink temperament and handling

**Skink temperament varies by species and by individual animal.** Some captive skinks become tolerant of routine handling, while others remain shy, defensive, or strongly oriented toward hiding and escape.

Blue-tongue skinks are commonly selected by people who want a larger terrestrial species that can become accustomed to calm interaction. Fire skinks are more likely to spend time under substrate or cover. Red-eyed crocodile skinks are crepuscular, primarily solitary, and strongly cover-oriented, so they are better suited to low-disturbance observation than repeated handling.

Do not select a skink solely because a species is described online as “friendly” or “docile.” Handling tolerance can differ between individuals, and stress signals should take priority over the keeper’s desire for interaction.

For general handling, defensive behavior, and bite context, see [skink handling, taming and temperament](/skink-care/handling-bites-temperament/).

## How much space does a pet skink need?

**Enclosure needs depend on the species’ adult size and habitat use.** A large terrestrial blue-tongue skink needs a very different footprint from a small burrowing species, while an emerald tree skink or prehensile-tailed skink needs meaningful vertical structure.

This selection page does not duplicate exact enclosure dimensions because those values belong to species-specific care and setup pages. Before buying any skink, check:

- expected adult size;
- terrestrial, fossorial, arboreal, or semi-arboreal habitat use;
- amount and depth of substrate needed;
- climbing and hiding requirements;
- the space needed to create the correct environmental gradient.

If the enclosure required by the adult animal will not fit comfortably in your home, choose a different species before purchasing the animal.

## Pet skink diet: insectivore, omnivore or herbivore?

**Skinks do not share one universal captive diet.** Species in the family range from insectivorous forms to omnivores and strongly herbivorous species.

That difference matters when choosing a pet. A keeper who does not want to maintain live feeder insects should not select an insectivorous species. A large herbivorous or omnivorous skink requires a different shopping, preparation, and supplementation routine from a small insect-eating species.

Examples include:

- fire skinks and red-eyed crocodile skinks, which are primarily insect-eating captive species;
- blue-tongue skinks, which are omnivorous;
- prehensile-tailed skinks, which are herbivorous.

For food categories, feeding strategy, and species-specific routing, use [what skinks eat: diet and nutrition by species](/skink-care/diet/).

## How long do pet skinks live?

**A skink should be treated as a long-term pet, not an animal purchased for a short trial period.** Captive lifespan varies substantially by species and husbandry, and several commonly kept skinks can live for many years.

Before buying, check the lifespan of the exact species you are considering and think about future housing, moving, family changes, travel, and access to reptile veterinary care.

For species-by-species lifespan, size, growth, and maturity comparisons, see our [skink lifespan, size and growth guide](/pet-skinks/lifespan-size-growth/).

## What does a pet skink cost?

**The true cost of a pet skink is the animal plus the complete setup and years of ongoing care.** Purchase price varies by species, availability, locality, captive-bred status, age, and morph, so a single “average skink price” is not useful for every species.

Budget for:

- the animal itself;
- an appropriately sized enclosure;
- heating, lighting, thermostatic control, and monitoring equipment;
- substrate, hides, furnishings, and water/feeding equipment;
- food and supplementation;
- replacement bulbs or other consumables where required;
- electricity;
- veterinary examinations and unexpected medical care.

A lower purchase price does not necessarily mean a cheaper pet. Specialized enclosure requirements, live food, electricity, or veterinary needs can make long-term ownership more expensive than the initial animal.

For prices, breeder evaluation, setup budgeting, and buying checks, see the [skink cost and buying guide](/pet-skinks/cost-buying-guide/).

## Captive-bred, rescue or wild-caught?

**Where suitable captive-bred animals are available, they are generally preferable for a prospective pet keeper because their origin and captive history can be easier to establish.** Imported or wild-caught reptiles can arrive with additional health, stress, acclimation, or conservation concerns, and these risks differ by species and source.

Do not rely on a seller label alone. Ask about:

- whether the animal was captive-bred, captive-hatched, imported, or field-collected;
- feeding history;
- approximate age and origin;
- previous health problems;
- current body condition and behavior;
- whether the seller can explain the species’ care requirements accurately.

Species with conservation or trade concerns deserve additional scrutiny. The prehensile-tailed skink evidence in the cited source material, for example, describes serious problems associated with historic importation and emphasizes captive breeding and veterinary care.

The [skink cost and buying guide](/pet-skinks/cost-buying-guide/) covers breeder and purchase evaluation in more detail.

## Can pet skinks live together?

**Do not assume two skinks can safely share an enclosure simply because they are the same species.** Social behavior varies across Scincidae. Some species or populations show social grouping, while many captive skinks are managed individually to reduce conflict, competition, stress, and bite injuries.

Only attempt group housing when reliable species-specific evidence supports it and the enclosure design allows animals to avoid one another, access resources, and be separated immediately if needed.

If a species care guide does not explicitly support cohabitation, individual housing is the safer default for planning purposes.

## How to choose the right pet skink

Before buying, compare the species against your real constraints:

1. **Space:** Can you house the adult animal, not just the juvenile?
2. **Environment:** Can you maintain its required heat, lighting, humidity, and substrate conditions?
3. **Diet:** Are the correct foods available to you every week?
4. **Interaction:** Do you want a handling-tolerant animal or are you happy with an observation species?
5. **Activity:** Would you rather watch a visible terrestrial skink, a climber, or a species that spends much of its time buried?
6. **Time:** Are you prepared for a multi-year commitment?
7. **Budget:** Can you cover the enclosure, equipment, food, electricity, and reptile veterinary care?
8. **Source:** Can you verify where the animal came from and whether its acquisition is responsible?

If those questions reveal a mismatch, choose another species before investing in the setup.

## Frequently asked questions
### What is the easiest pet skink to care for?

Blue-tongue skinks are commonly considered a practical starting point for keepers who can provide the required adult space and husbandry. “Easy” is relative, however: no skink is appropriate if its enclosure, diet, climate, or veterinary needs exceed the keeper’s resources.

### Are pet skinks friendly?

Some individuals become calm and tolerant of routine handling, while others remain shy or defensive. Temperament depends on species, individual history, health, and the way the animal is approached.

### Are skinks good pets for children?

A skink should be treated as an adult-managed exotic pet rather than a low-maintenance child’s pet. Husbandry equipment, long-term costs, hygiene, handling, and veterinary responsibility require consistent adult supervision.

### Should I buy a skink before setting up the enclosure?

No. Choose the species first, research its exact needs, prepare and test the enclosure, and only then acquire the animal from a responsible source.
