# Shingleback Skink: Tiliqua rugosa, Lifelong Pair Bonds, Size & Care Context

**The shingleback skink (*Tiliqua rugosa*) is a heavy-bodied Australian blue-tongue relative known for its rough, enlarged scales, short thick tail, slow terrestrial movement, and unusually persistent pair associations.** This page is primarily a species and life-history guide, not a full husbandry manual.

For the genus context, see [blue-tongue skink types](/pet-skinks/blue-tongue-skink/types/).

## What is a shingleback skink?

*Shinglebacks are a distinct species within the genus Tiliqua.* Common names include shingleback, sleepy lizard, stumpytail, and pinecone lizard.

Published species accounts describe:

- **SVL: roughly 260–290 mm;**
- **weight: up to around 800 g** in the available sources;
- a short, thick tail;
- heavy, rugose scales.

The tail resembles the head in overall bulk, which may complicate a predator’s attack direction, but the page should avoid presenting this as a proven single-purpose adaptation without a direct behavioral source.

## Pair bonding

**Long-term pair fidelity in *Tiliqua rugosa* is well documented and is one of the species’ defining behavioral traits.**

Available research describes males and females reuniting across breeding seasons and maintaining repeated pair associations over many years.

“Lifelong pair bond” is a useful summary of the species’ reputation, but it should not be interpreted to mean that every wild animal forms an unbreakable pair for its entire lifespan.

The strongest wording is:

> Shinglebacks show unusually persistent, long-term monogamous pair associations, with some pairs reuniting across many breeding seasons.

## Reproduction

Shinglebacks are live-bearing. Published natural-history accounts describe small litters, often one or two very large young.

This reproductive strategy fits the page’s life-history focus but does not need a full breeding protocol.

## Habitat and activity

Shinglebacks are terrestrial Australian skinks associated with dry shrubland, grassland, woodland, and semi-arid habitat depending on population and subspecies.

They are diurnal and often move relatively slowly compared with smaller skinks.

## Diet

A species-specific captive diet should use a reliable *T. rugosa* husbandry source rather than simply copying the RSPCA *T. scincoides* ratio.

## Captive-care context

Because this is a **Species Guide** rather than a dedicated husbandry page, exact enclosure, UVI, substrate depth, and supplement schedules should not dominate it.

Captive care should use a large terrestrial enclosure, a measured thermal gradient, secure hides, and locality-appropriate humidity rather than copying Northern blue-tongue values automatically.

Core care needs include:

- substantial terrestrial floor space;
- secure hides;
- controlled overhead heat;
- UVB;
- dry-to-moderate species-appropriate conditions;
- an omnivorous diet with strong plant representation.

For general implementation, use the [skink care guide](/skink-care/) and [skink enclosure guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## Can shinglebacks be kept in pairs?

**Wild pair fidelity does not automatically prove that every captive pair will be compatible in one enclosure.**

If captive pair housing is attempted, it should be based on:

- individual compatibility;
- adequate enclosure space;
- duplicate resources;
- careful introduction;
- observation for exclusion or aggression;
- immediate separation capacity.

Do not use wild monogamy as a shortcut to a universal cohabitation recommendation.

## Frequently asked questions
### Are shinglebacks monogamous?

They show exceptionally persistent long-term pair fidelity, with documented pairs reuniting across breeding seasons.

### Are shinglebacks blue-tongue skinks?

They belong to the genus *Tiliqua* and are part of the blue-tongue skink group, but *Tiliqua rugosa* is its own species.

### What is unusual about the tail?

It is short, thick, and head-like in overall bulk compared with the long tapered tails of many lizards.

### Do they need the same care as Northern blue-tongues?

No. They are related, but species-specific climate, diet, and enclosure guidance should be used rather than automatically copying *T. scincoides* values.
