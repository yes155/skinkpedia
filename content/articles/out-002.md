# Five-Lined Skink Guide: Identification, Range, Diet, Sex Differences & Life Cycle

**The Common Five-lined Skink (*Plestiodon fasciatus*) is a North American skink identified by age-dependent striping, a bright blue juvenile tail, and strong differences between juveniles, adult females, and breeding males.** In Ontario it is especially important because it is the province’s only native lizard and occurs in two conservation units with different status.

This guide follows **identification → sex/age differences → range → diet → reproduction**. The broader “blue-tailed skink” common-name problem belongs on the [Blue-Tailed Skink Guide](/wild-skinks/blue-tailed-skink/). For regional identification across the United States and Canada, use the [North American Skinks directory](/wild-skinks/north-america/).

## How do you identify a five-lined skink?

**Juveniles are dark with five pale longitudinal stripes and a vivid blue tail.** Appearance changes substantially with age.

Typical life-stage pattern:

- **Juvenile:** dark body, five light stripes, bright blue tail;
- **Adult female:** often retains more of the striped pattern;
- **Adult male:** tends to become more uniformly bronze or brown;
- **Breeding male:** develops enlarged reddish-orange jaws and cheeks.

Because Southeastern Five-lined Skinks and young Broad-headed Skinks can look similar, location and scale characters matter.

## Five-lined skink male vs female

**Adult males usually lose more of the juvenile striping and develop enlarged orange-red jaw coloration during the breeding season, while females tend to retain a stronger striped pattern.**

This difference is easiest to recognize in mature animals. Juveniles of both sexes may look very similar.

Color alone is not enough for identification outside the known geographic range.

## Where do five-lined skinks live?

**Common Five-lined Skinks occupy much of eastern North America, including southern Ontario and a broad area of the eastern United States.**

Habitats can include:

- deciduous woodland;
- rocky openings;
- forest edges;
- areas with logs, bark, rocks, or other ground cover;
- suitable nesting sites with protected moisture.

The species’ Ontario populations are especially well studied.

## Ontario conservation status

**Ontario recognizes two geographically separated Common Five-lined Skink populations with different conservation status.**

The published evidence identifies:

- **Carolinian population — Endangered**
- **Southern Shield population — Special Concern**

The Carolinian population occurs in southwestern Ontario, while the Southern Shield population is associated with rocky shield habitat farther north and east.

Conservation status is jurisdiction-specific, so these Ontario designations should not be presented as the species’ global status.

## How big do five-lined skinks get?

**Common Five-lined Skinks are medium-sized among North American skinks and remain smaller than the largest Broad-headed Skinks.**

Published references use an upper total length of roughly **8.5 inches** for *P. fasciatus*. Exact body size varies with sex, age, and population.

## What do five-lined skinks eat?

**Five-lined skinks are insectivorous and feed on a range of small invertebrates.**

Published evidence lists prey such as:

- beetles;
- spiders;
- crickets;
- caterpillars;
- insect larvae;
- other small arthropods.

A biomechanical dataset used in the cited sources reports an average bite force of about **6.58 ± 1.51 N** for *P. fasciatus*. That figure is a comparative measurement, not a measure of how dangerous the animal is to people.

## Predators and defense

**Five-lined skinks are prey for snakes, birds, mammals, and other predators.** Their juvenile blue tail and ability to shed the tail form part of their defensive system.

If grabbed, the skink may detach the tail, allowing the animal to escape while the detached section continues moving.

## Tail loss and regeneration

**Tail autotomy can save the animal during a predator attack, but regrowing the tail requires energy.**

The tail can also function as an energy-storage site. That makes tail loss biologically costly, particularly where animals must prepare for seasonal inactivity.

For the general mechanism, see [skink tail drop and regrowth](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/).

## Five-lined skink reproduction and eggs

**Common Five-lined Skinks are egg-laying, and females remain associated with the nest during incubation.**

Females use protected nest sites beneath cover or within suitable cavities. Maternal attendance in *Plestiodon* can help maintain nest conditions and protect eggs.

Detailed cross-species reproductive biology belongs in the [skink reproduction guide](/skink-biology/reproduction/).

## Five-lined vs Southeastern Five-lined Skink

**These species can be difficult to separate visually, but scale characters on the underside of the tail help with identification.**

The published evidence notes:

- *P. inexpectatus* has more equal-sized subcaudal scales;
- *P. fasciatus* has a widened median row beneath the tail.

Geography should also be considered before attempting identification from close-up scale features.

## Five-lined vs Broad-headed Skink

Broad-headed Skinks become substantially larger, and mature males develop a broader, more massive head.

Juveniles can be difficult to distinguish because both may have dark bodies, stripes, and bright blue tails.

For the dedicated comparison entity, see the [Broad-headed Skink guide](/wild-skinks/broad-headed-skink/).

## Are five-lined skinks poisonous?

**They are not treated as venomous skinks in the published evidence.** Avoid expanding this page into general dog/cat ingestion advice; that user task belongs on [Are Skinks Poisonous or Dangerous?](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

## Frequently asked questions
### What does a juvenile five-lined skink look like?

A juvenile typically has a dark body, five pale stripes, and a vivid blue tail.

### How can you tell a male five-lined skink from a female?

Adult males tend to lose more of the striping and develop enlarged orange-red jaws during the breeding season. Females usually retain more of the striped pattern.

### What do five-lined skinks eat?

They eat small invertebrates such as beetles, spiders, crickets, caterpillars, and insect larvae.

### Do five-lined skink tails grow back?

They can regenerate a lost tail, but the new tail differs from the original and regrowth carries an energetic cost.

### Are five-lined skinks poisonous?

They are not treated as venomous skinks. Broader pet-safety and toxicity myths are covered in the [skink safety guide](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

### What is the conservation status of the five-lined skink in Ontario?

The published evidence identifies the Carolinian population as Endangered and the Southern Shield population as Special Concern.
