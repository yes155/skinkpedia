# Skink Biology: Anatomy, Classification, Scientific Names & Unusual Adaptations

**Skinks are lizards in the family Scincidae, one of the largest and most ecologically diverse lizard families.** The Reptile Database currently lists **1,817 species** in Scincidae, and the total changes as new species are described and taxonomic revisions are published.

This guide follows **taxonomy → anatomy → physiology → unusual adaptations**. Reproduction and tail autotomy have dedicated child pages.

## What is the scientific classification of a skink?

At a stable high level:

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Phylum:** Chordata
- **Class:** Reptilia
- **Order:** Squamata
- **Superfamily:** Scincoidea
- **Family:** Scincidae

The family name **Scincidae** dates to Oppel, 1811.

For the broad, nontechnical definition, see [What Is a Skink?](/skinks/).

## How many species of skink are there?

**About 1,800 recognized species** is the appropriate current order-of-magnitude statement; the Reptile Database currently lists **1,817**.

Exact totals change because:

- new species continue to be described;
- genetic studies split or merge taxa;
- genera and subfamilies are revised.

The earlier “over 1,500 species and more than 150 genera” wording is no longer the best current summary.

## What makes a skink a skink?

There is no single body shape shared by every species, but common Scincidae traits include:

- overlapping scales, often with underlying osteoderms;
- a generally elongate body plan in many lineages;
- substantial diversity in limb length;
- repeated evolution of burrowing, climbing, terrestrial and rock-dwelling forms.

Some skinks look like conventional four-legged lizards, while others have tiny limbs or no visible limbs.

## Skink osteoderms

**Many skinks have bony plates called osteoderms associated with the skin and scales.**

Research on skink integument shows that osteoderm structure is more complex than simply describing every scale as a single “armor plate.” Form, arrangement and development vary among lineages.

Osteoderms can contribute to protection and structural support without making the animal rigid.

## Why have some skinks lost their legs?

**Limb reduction has evolved repeatedly and independently in Scincidae.**

It is especially common in lineages adapted to moving through:

- loose soil;
- sand;
- leaf litter;
- narrow underground spaces.

Evolutionary changes can involve shorter limbs, fewer digits, body elongation and increased vertebral number.

The [Florida Sand Skink](/wild-skinks/florida-sand-skink/) is an extreme North American example.

## Skink head shape and bite performance

Comparative biomechanics show that head dimensions influence jaw mechanics and bite performance.

In general, broader or taller heads can provide more room for jaw musculature, but the relationship between skull shape, ecology and diet is not one simple “herbivore vs insectivore” rule.

Comparative bite-force datasets are best used for species comparisons, such as:

- Blue-tongue skink;
- Broad-headed Skink;
- Red-eyed crocodile skink.

Avoid turning those measurements into claims about aggressiveness.

## Why do some skinks have green blood?

**Green-blooded skinks accumulate exceptionally high concentrations of the bile pigment biliverdin.**

Historically these species were grouped in *Prasinohaema*. A 2025 genome and phylogenetic study notes that “*Prasinohaema*” is polyphyletic and that green blood appears to have evolved **four independent times** within the relevant New Guinea skink radiation.

The green coloration can extend beyond the blood to internal tissues.

This is one of the most unusual known physiological traits in amniotes.

## Do all skinks have the same reproductive system?

No. Scincidae includes both egg-laying and live-bearing species, with repeated independent evolution of viviparity.

That complexity belongs on the dedicated [skink reproduction guide](/skink-biology/reproduction/).

## Do all skinks drop their tails?

No. Caudal autotomy is widespread but not identical across every species.

The detailed mechanics, regrowth structure and care implications belong on [Why Do Skinks Drop Their Tails?](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/).

## Skink ecological diversity

Skinks occupy a remarkable range of ecological niches.

Examples include:

- **fossorial:** Florida Sand Skink, Sandfish Skink;
- **arboreal:** Emerald Tree Skink, Prehensile-tailed Skink;
- **terrestrial:** Blue-tongue Skinks, Fire Skink;
- **saxicolous:** Gidgee Skink and other rock-associated lineages.

They occur across tropical, temperate, arid and montane environments on every continent except Antarctica.

## Scientific names and taxonomy change over time

Older sources may use names that have since changed.

Examples already encountered in the cited source material include:

- *Eumeces septentrionalis* → ***Plestiodon septentrionalis***
- *Mabuya longicaudata* → ***Eutropis longicaudata***
- *Lepidothyris fernandi* → ***Mochlus fernandi*** in available sources usage

A publication-quality species page should therefore verify the accepted scientific name against a current taxonomic database rather than copying an older care sheet blindly.

## Frequently asked questions
### What is the scientific name for skinks?

The family scientific name is **Scincidae**. Individual skink species each have their own genus and species name.

### How are skinks classified?

Skinks are reptiles in the order Squamata, superfamily Scincoidea, family Scincidae.

### How many skink species are there?

The Reptile Database currently lists 1,817 species, with the exact total changing as taxonomy is updated.

### Do skinks have bones under their scales?

Many skinks possess osteoderms—bony structures associated with the skin and scales.

### Why are some skinks almost legless?

Limb reduction has evolved repeatedly in burrowing lineages where an elongated body can help movement through soil, sand, or litter.

### Are limbless skinks snakes?

No. They are lizards in Scincidae despite their snake-like body shape.

### Why do some skinks have green blood?

High biliverdin concentrations make the blood green. Recent research indicates that the trait evolved independently multiple times.

### What is a green-blooded skink?

The name refers to New Guinea skink lineages historically grouped under *Prasinohaema* that accumulate extraordinary biliverdin levels.

### Do skinks lay eggs?

Some do; others give birth to live young. See the [skink reproduction guide](/skink-biology/reproduction/).

### Can skinks regrow their tails?

Many can, but the replacement tail differs anatomically from the original. See the [tail autotomy guide](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/).
