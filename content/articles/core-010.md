# Skink Health Guide: Shedding, Weight, Mites, Respiratory Issues & Vet Signs

**Skink health problems are often first noticed as changes in shedding, breathing, body condition, activity, appetite, or the appearance of the skin.** Good husbandry can reduce preventable problems, but this page is for **recognition and veterinary escalation**, not for diagnosing disease or prescribing medication at home.

For the broader husbandry framework, see the [skink care guide](/skink-care/). Diet-specific prevention belongs in the [skink diet guide](/skink-care/diet/), while tail injuries and regrowth belong in the [skink tail drop and regrowth guide](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/).

## What does a healthy skink look like?

**A healthy skink should be assessed by its normal species-specific pattern rather than by one universal checklist.** The most useful signs are consistency over time: normal breathing, expected activity, stable body condition, normal feeding behavior, intact skin, and appropriate shedding.

Routine observations can include:

- breathing effort;
- appetite and feeding pattern;
- body weight or body-condition trend;
- skin and scale condition;
- shedding;
- eyes and nostrils;
- movement and posture;
- behavior;
- fecal changes;
- use of water and hides.

A sudden change from an individual skink’s normal pattern can matter even when no single symptom is diagnostic.

## Preventive husbandry and health monitoring

**Correct temperature, humidity, lighting, diet, enclosure hygiene, and species-appropriate care reduce the risk of husbandry-related illness.** These variables interact, so a health problem should not be reduced automatically to one cause.

Useful preventive checks include:

1. verify temperature and humidity against the exact species’ care guidance;
2. confirm that heat and lighting equipment are functioning correctly;
3. review diet and supplementation for the species and life stage;
4. keep drinking water and food-contact surfaces clean;
5. remove obvious waste and spoiled food promptly;
6. monitor weight and body condition over time;
7. watch for changes in breathing, shedding, movement, appetite, or behavior.

Do not assume that adjusting one husbandry variable will treat an established medical problem.

## Metabolic bone disease: warning signs and prevention

**Metabolic bone disease is a broad husbandry-associated skeletal disorder in reptiles, and nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism is one important form discussed in the published evidence.** Problems with calcium balance, nutrition, and inadequate species-appropriate UVB exposure can contribute to abnormal bone mineralization.

Warning signs described in the source material include:

- jaw softening or abnormal jaw shape;
- weakness;
- difficulty moving normally;
- abnormal curvature or deformation of the skeleton;
- declining body condition.

These signs require veterinary assessment. They should not be treated by simply increasing calcium powder or changing a UVB lamp without evaluating the animal and the complete husbandry system.

### Prevention

Prevention depends on:

- an appropriate diet;
- species-specific supplementation;
- species-appropriate UVB exposure where required;
- correct enclosure temperatures;
- monitoring growth and body condition.

For feeding and supplementation boundaries, see [what skinks eat](/skink-care/diet/). For installation and environmental monitoring, see the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## Abnormal shedding and retained shed

**Incomplete or retained shedding, often called dysecdysis, can be associated with inappropriate humidity, dehydration, enclosure conditions, or other health problems.** The correct humidity target depends on species, so a dry-adapted skink and a humid forest species should not be managed with the same moisture level.

Pay particular attention to retained skin around:

- toes;
- tail tip;
- limbs;
- eyes or face;
- other narrow areas where constricting shed may persist.

Retained shed that is tight, persistent, associated with swelling, discoloration, pain, or tissue damage deserves veterinary attention.

### What to check first

Review:

- species-specific humidity;
- access to water;
- substrate moisture where relevant;
- availability of appropriate shelter or humid microclimates where the species requires them;
- recent changes in heating or ventilation;
- whether the skin is actually retained rather than simply part of an active shed cycle.

Avoid pulling dry, firmly attached skin away by force.

## Respiratory warning signs

**Labored breathing, wheezing, clicking, open-mouth breathing, or mucus and bubbles around the nostrils can indicate a respiratory problem and warrant prompt veterinary evaluation.**

Cited sources associate respiratory problems with husbandry issues such as:

- inappropriate temperatures;
- poor ventilation;
- persistently wet or unsuitable enclosure conditions.

Those associations do not prove the cause in an individual animal. Similar signs can require professional examination, so the safest response is to check the enclosure against the species’ requirements and arrange reptile-veterinary assessment.

Do not begin antibiotics or other medication without veterinary direction.

## External mites and other visible parasites

**Small red or dark parasites around the eyes, ear openings, skin folds, or other thin-skinned areas may indicate an external parasite problem.** Cited sources also describe prolonged soaking as a behavior sometimes seen in reptiles with mite infestations.

Possible signs include:

- visible moving specks on the animal;
- repeated rubbing;
- increased irritation or restlessness;
- unusual time spent soaking;
- skin damage in heavily affected areas.

These signs are not specific enough to identify the parasite species reliably at home.

### What to do if you suspect mites

- isolate the affected animal from other reptiles where practical;
- inspect the enclosure and furnishings;
- avoid moving substrate or equipment between enclosures;
- contact a reptile veterinarian for identification and treatment guidance;
- clean and manage the enclosure according to the treatment plan.

Do not apply unverified pesticides, household insecticides, essential oils, or arbitrary mite treatments directly to a skink.

## Weight loss and body-condition changes

**Unexpected weight loss is an important health warning, especially when it occurs with reduced appetite, weakness, abnormal feces, or behavioral change.**

Weight loss can have many possible explanations, and the cited sources specifically caution against assuming one diagnosis from the sign alone.

A useful monitoring approach is to:

- weigh the skink consistently where handling permits;
- record the date and weight;
- note appetite;
- record major sheds;
- note reproductive status where relevant;
- watch for changes in muscle and overall body condition.

The trend matters more than one isolated number.

Persistent weight loss, marked wasting, or weight loss combined with other signs should prompt veterinary assessment.

For normal growth and size tracking, see the [skink lifespan, size and growth guide](/pet-skinks/lifespan-size-growth/).

## Loss of appetite

**A skink eating less than usual is a sign to investigate, not a diagnosis by itself.** Appetite can change with species, life stage, season, reproductive condition, environment, stress, and illness.

Check:

- enclosure temperature;
- recent changes in humidity or lighting;
- food type and feeding routine;
- recent transport or handling stress;
- body-weight trend;
- fecal changes;
- other physical or behavioral signs.

If appetite loss persists or is accompanied by weight loss, weakness, respiratory signs, swelling, or another concerning change, contact a reptile veterinarian.

## Brumation vs. illness

**Reduced activity during a normal seasonal slowdown should not automatically be assumed to be illness, but lethargy should not automatically be dismissed as brumation either.**

Brumation behavior is species-specific. The source material discusses seasonal slowdown particularly in blue-tongue skinks, so a blue-tongue brumation pattern should not be generalized across all Scincidae.

Consider veterinary advice when an inactive skink also shows:

- marked or continuing weight loss;
- weakness;
- abnormal breathing;
- poor body condition;
- sunken appearance;
- inability to move normally;
- other signs inconsistent with the animal’s usual seasonal pattern.

Do not deliberately induce brumation using generic temperatures or schedules from another species.

## Skin lesions, wounds and suspected “scale rot”

**Discolored scales, ulcers, swelling, open wounds, burns, or persistently damaged skin require a careful husbandry review and may need veterinary diagnosis.**

The term “scale rot” is used loosely in reptile-keeping discussions and should not be treated as one specific diagnosis. Skin disease can involve different causes, including trauma, environmental problems, and infection.

If you notice a skin lesion:

- keep the enclosure clean;
- correct obvious husbandry problems without creating a sudden extreme change;
- prevent further trauma;
- photograph the area to document progression;
- seek veterinary assessment if the lesion is open, spreading, swollen, painful-looking, or not improving.

Do not start antibiotics, antifungal products, or strong antiseptics without professional guidance.

## Burns and heat-source injuries

**Burns can occur when a skink contacts an unsafe heat source or when the enclosure reaches temperatures beyond the species’ safe range.**

Possible warning signs include:

- discolored skin;
- blistering;
- damaged scales;
- open wounds;
- avoidance of movement;
- sudden behavior changes after a heating failure.

If a burn is suspected, remove access to the hazardous heat source and contact a reptile veterinarian. Do not apply household creams or topical medication unless directed.

Heat sources should be regulated and installed so the animal cannot make unsafe contact. See the [skink enclosure setup guide](/skink-care/enclosure-setup/).

## Tail injuries and tail loss

**Tail loss can be a natural defensive response in species capable of autotomy, but traumatic injury, bleeding, swelling, or abnormal healing still requires attention.**

Do not pull retained tissue, cut the tail, or apply medication without guidance.

For the biology of autotomy and normal regrowth, use the [skink tail drop and regrowth guide](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/). If the tail is injured rather than cleanly shed, or if the site becomes swollen, discolored, persistently bleeding, or otherwise abnormal, seek veterinary assessment.

## Cat and dog injuries

**Any bite or claw injury from a cat or dog should be taken seriously because even a small external wound can hide deeper trauma or contamination.**

Keep the skink away from domestic predators and seek veterinary advice promptly after a bite or significant scratch, especially if there is:

- puncture trauma;
- bleeding;
- swelling;
- breathing difficulty;
- weakness;
- reduced movement.

Do not rely on the absence of a large visible wound to conclude that the animal is unharmed.

Questions about whether skinks are poisonous or dangerous to pets belong in [are skinks poisonous or dangerous?](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

## When to contact a reptile veterinarian

**Seek veterinary advice promptly when a skink has persistent or serious changes that go beyond routine husbandry troubleshooting.**

Important escalation signs include:

- labored, noisy, or open-mouth breathing;
- mucus or bubbles around the nostrils;
- sudden or continuing weight loss;
- severe weakness or inability to move normally;
- jaw softening or skeletal deformity;
- open wounds, burns, or major swelling;
- persistent retained shed with tissue damage;
- visible parasites with declining condition;
- prolonged appetite loss with weight loss;
- severe lethargy or unresponsiveness;
- eye or mouth abnormalities;
- any rapid deterioration.

A reptile-experienced veterinarian can examine the animal, assess husbandry, and decide whether diagnostic testing or treatment is necessary.

## What not to treat at home

**This page should help you recognize problems, not turn symptoms into do-it-yourself medical protocols.**

Do not independently prescribe or dose:

- antibiotics;
- antiparasitic medication;
- dewormers;
- prescription antiseptics;
- injectable medications;
- treatment-level vitamin or calcium doses.

Do not use a brand-name product simply because it appears in a reptile forum, seller recommendation, or affiliate listing.

Medical treatment belongs under veterinary direction.

## Preparing for a veterinary visit

Bring information that helps the veterinarian understand both the animal and its environment:

- exact species;
- age if known;
- source and acquisition date;
- current diet;
- supplements;
- enclosure dimensions;
- temperature measurements;
- humidity measurements where relevant;
- UVB equipment where used;
- recent weight records;
- appetite changes;
- photographs of lesions or abnormal shed;
- fecal or shedding history;
- medications or products already used.

This can help distinguish a husbandry problem from a condition that requires further diagnosis.

## Frequently asked questions
### How can I tell if my skink is sick?

Look for a change from the animal’s normal pattern. Persistent appetite loss, weight loss, abnormal breathing, weakness, retained shed with tissue problems, visible parasites, wounds, swelling, or major behavioral change should prompt closer evaluation and, when significant, veterinary advice.

### What are signs of a respiratory infection in a skink?

Cited sources identify labored breathing, wheezing or clicking, open-mouth breathing, and mucus or bubbles around the nostrils as important respiratory warning signs. These signs need veterinary assessment rather than home diagnosis.

### How do I treat mites on a skink?

Do not use an arbitrary pesticide or medication. Isolate the affected reptile where practical, prevent equipment transfer between enclosures, and seek veterinary guidance for identification, animal treatment, and enclosure management.

### Is retained shed dangerous?

Persistent retained shed can damage tissue, particularly in narrow areas such as toes or the tail tip. Review species-appropriate humidity and hydration, and seek veterinary help if the shed is constricting, painful-looking, discolored, or not resolving.

### Is my skink brumating or sick?

Brumation is species-specific. If reduced activity is accompanied by weight loss, weakness, respiratory signs, poor body condition, or another abnormal change, do not assume it is normal seasonal behavior.
