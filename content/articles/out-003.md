# Broad-Headed Skink Guide: Identification, Range, Size, Diet & Male vs Female

**The Broad-headed Skink (*Plestiodon laticeps*) is one of the largest North American skinks and is especially recognizable in adulthood because breeding males develop a broad orange-red head.** The species is more arboreal than many smaller *Plestiodon* and commonly uses mature hardwood forest with standing trees, cavities, logs, and ground cover.

This guide follows **morphology → sex differences → range → ecology**. Broader North American distribution belongs on [North American Skinks](/wild-skinks/north-america/).

## How big is a Broad-headed Skink?

**Adults can reach approximately 8–13 inches (20–33 cm) total length.**

Comparative morphology data also report an average snout-vent length of roughly **99.9 mm**, excluding the tail.

That makes *P. laticeps* considerably larger-bodied than the Common Five-lined Skink.

## How do you identify a Broad-headed Skink?

Identification depends heavily on age and sex.

### Juveniles

Young Broad-headed Skinks can have:

- dark bodies;
- five pale stripes;
- bright blue tails.

This makes them easy to confuse with juvenile five-lined skinks.

### Adult females

Females usually retain more striping than mature males and do not develop the same massive orange breeding head.

### Adult males

**Mature breeding males develop the defining broad head and orange-to-red head coloration.**

The head becomes especially conspicuous during the breeding season, when jaw musculature and head dimensions are more pronounced.

## Broad-headed skink male vs female

**The easiest adult difference is the male’s enlarged head and seasonal orange-red coloration.**

Females are generally slimmer-headed and more likely to retain longitudinal body stripes.

Sexing juveniles from casual photographs is much less reliable.

## Where do Broad-headed Skinks live?

**The species occurs in the southeastern United States and is strongly associated with mature woodland.**

Cited sources emphasize:

- hardwood forest;
- large trees;
- tree cavities;
- logs and woody cover;
- forest-edge structure.

Broad-headed Skinks are often described as semi-arboreal because they climb trees more readily than many related ground-oriented skinks.

They should not, however, be described as spending their entire active season “high in the canopy” unless a local study directly supports that wording.

## What do Broad-headed Skinks eat?

**They feed on insects and other small animal prey, including relatively hard-bodied invertebrates.**

Published references list prey such as:

- beetles;
- crickets;
- spiders;
- snails;
- other arthropods.

The species’ robust head and jaw anatomy are consistent with stronger bite performance than smaller *Plestiodon*.

## Broad-headed skink bite force

**A comparative biomechanical dataset reports an average bite force of approximately 17.86 ± 4.26 N.**

The same dataset gives approximate head measurements of:

- head length: **19.59 ± 2.56 mm**
- jaw length: **15.09 ± 2.84 mm**

These are research measurements, not practical bite-injury predictions.

A wild animal may bite defensively if captured, so observation is preferable to unnecessary handling.

## Reproduction and nest guarding

**Broad-headed Skinks are egg-laying and females attend their nests.**

Available research gives a clutch size of roughly **8–15 eggs** and describes females remaining with the clutch in protected nest cavities.

Maternal attendance is important because it differentiates this reproductive strategy from small skinks such as *Scincella lateralis*, which do not guard their eggs.

The page should not overstate nest defense by implying every female aggressively attacks any predator.

## Broad-headed vs Five-lined Skink

These two species overlap in appearance most strongly when young.

Useful differences include:

| Trait | Broad-headed Skink | Common Five-lined Skink |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Adult size** | Larger; up to about 13 in | Smaller; published material uses up to about 8.5 in |
| **Adult male head** | Very broad and orange-red in breeding season | Reddish jaws, but head less massive |
| **Lifestyle** | More strongly associated with climbing and large trees | More ground/cover oriented |
| **Juveniles** | Striped with blue tail | Striped with blue tail |

When uncertain, geography and scale characters are more reliable than color alone.

## Are Broad-headed Skinks poisonous?

**The published evidence does not classify Broad-headed Skinks as venomous.** Their defensive bite can still be painful, so “non-venomous” should not be rewritten as “zero danger.”

General poison myths, dogs, cats, and bite-safety questions belong on the [skink safety page](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

## Can Broad-headed Skinks be kept as pets?

Wildlife laws and collection rules vary by location. This wild-species page should not encourage capture.

Readers interested in captive skinks should use the [pet skink selection guide](/pet-skinks/) and select a legally available captive-bred species with established care information.

## Frequently asked questions
### How big do Broad-headed Skinks get?

Adults can reach roughly 8–13 inches total length.

### How can you tell a male Broad-headed Skink from a female?

Breeding males develop a much broader head and orange-red coloration. Females typically retain more body striping.

### Do Broad-headed Skinks have blue tails?

Juveniles can have vivid blue tails. The coloration becomes less prominent as they mature.

### How hard can a Broad-headed Skink bite?

The comparative biomechanical dataset reports an average bite force of about 17.86 N. That is a comparative research value, not a guaranteed injury level.

### What do Broad-headed Skinks eat?

They eat insects and other small prey, including beetles, crickets, spiders, and snails.

### Are Broad-headed Skinks poisonous?

They are not treated as venomous skinks in the published evidence. Broader safety questions belong on the [skink poison and danger guide](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).
