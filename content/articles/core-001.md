# Skink: What It Is, Types, Habitat, Size & Key Facts

A **skink** is a lizard in the family **Scincidae**, part of the order Squamata. Skinks are known for smooth, overlapping scales, elongated bodies, and a wide range of lifestyles, from ground-dwelling and burrowing species to tree-climbing and rock-dwelling forms. They occur across much of the world and vary greatly in size, diet, limb development, and behavior.

If you are considering a skink as a pet rather than learning about the family as a whole, see our [guide to keeping pet skinks](/pet-skinks/).

## What is a skink?

**A skink is a lizard belonging to the family Scincidae.** Many skinks have smooth, glossy-looking scales and relatively elongated bodies. Bony plates called osteoderms occur beneath the scales in the family and contribute to the firm, armored feel associated with many species.

Skinks are highly diverse. Some have four well-developed legs, others have shortened limbs, and some burrowing lineages have evolved extreme limb reduction. This is one reason certain skinks can look superficially snake-like even though they are lizards.

For deeper coverage of skink anatomy, classification, osteoderms, and unusual physiological traits, see our [skink biology guide](/skink-biology/).

## Are skinks lizards or snakes?

**Skinks are lizards, not snakes.** They belong to Squamata, the same reptile order that also contains snakes, but skinks are members of the lizard family Scincidae.

Their long bodies, smooth scales, and reduced limbs can make some species resemble snakes at first glance. Other skinks are more robust and clearly four-legged. Body form varies with ecology, especially in species adapted for burrowing through soil, sand, or leaf litter.

## What do skinks look like?

Skinks share several recurring traits, but there is no single body shape that fits every species.

| Feature | Common pattern in skinks | Important variation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scales** | Smooth, overlapping scales, often with underlying osteoderms | Texture and scale form vary among lineages |
| **Body shape** | Frequently elongated and cylindrical | Some species are broad-bodied and heavy |
| **Limbs** | Often relatively short | Limb reduction ranges from mild to complete in some burrowing lineages |
| **Head and neck** | Head often merges gradually into the body | Head shape varies with diet and ecology |
| **Lifestyle** | Many are terrestrial or fossorial | Arboreal, saxicolous, and semi-aquatic forms also occur |

Some skinks also have specialized eyelid structures associated with burrowing, while other species retain more typical lizard eye anatomy.

## How big do skinks get?

**Skink size varies enormously by species.** In published comparative data, adult snout-to-vent length ranges from roughly **33 mm in very small skinks such as some *Scincella*** to **275 mm or more in large-bodied skinks such as *Corucia***. Total length can be much greater because the tail is not included in snout-to-vent measurements.

That range is useful for understanding the family, but pet-size comparisons and growth expectations belong in our dedicated [skink lifespan, size and growth guide](/pet-skinks/lifespan-size-growth/).

## Major types of skinks

There are many skink genera and species, but several ecological and familiar groups help illustrate how diverse the family is:

- **Blue-tongue skinks (*Tiliqua*)** are large-bodied terrestrial skinks represented by several species and regional forms.
- **Fire skinks** are terrestrial, burrowing skinks associated with forest-floor habitats.
- **Tree skinks** include climbing species adapted to elevated vegetation and vertical structure.
- **Crocodile skinks (*Tribolonotus*)** are forest-floor skinks with a distinctive armored appearance.
- **Sandfish skinks (*Scincus*)** are highly specialized for moving through loose desert sand.
- **Limb-reduced skinks** include fossorial lineages in which the legs have become very small or have been lost.

These are examples rather than a complete taxonomic list. Species profiles and care requirements are handled on their own pages so that the central skink page stays focused on the family as a whole.

## Where do skinks live?

**Skinks have a broad global distribution and occupy habitats ranging from deserts and beaches to rainforests, grasslands, temperate woodland, rocky slopes, and alpine environments.** The family is especially diverse in Australia, New Guinea, Southeast Asia, Oceania, Africa, and other warm regions.

Published research also documents skinks across a very wide elevational range, including records from near sea level to about **5,490 metres** in Himalayan alpine habitat.

Their lifestyles include:

- **Terrestrial:** living mainly on the ground among soil, grass, logs, and leaf litter.
- **Fossorial:** spending much of their time beneath sand or soil.
- **Arboreal:** climbing vegetation and using elevated cover.
- **Saxicolous:** living around rocks, cliffs, and crevices.
- **Semi-aquatic:** using stream margins or other wet habitats in species adapted to them.

For North American identification and state-by-state coverage, use our [North American skink directory](/wild-skinks/north-america/).

## What do skinks eat in the wild?

**Many small and medium-sized skinks feed mainly on invertebrates, while larger lineages can be omnivorous or strongly plant-eating.** Common prey includes insects and other arthropods, and some larger skinks also consume leaves, flowers, fruit, and other plant material.

Diet is closely related to body form. Research represented in comparative research links broader, taller heads with greater bite performance in skinks that process tougher foods, while narrower heads can favor rapid prey capture.

Captive feeding is a separate user task because nutritional requirements vary substantially by species. General pet-feeding guidance belongs in the [skink diet guide](/skink-care/diet/), while species pages provide narrower feeding recommendations.

## Why do skinks drop their tails?

**Many skinks can shed part of the tail as a defense against predators, a process called caudal autotomy.** The tail separates along specialized fracture zones, allowing the animal to escape while the detached tail continues moving and distracts the predator.

A replacement tail can grow after autotomy, but it is not an exact copy of the original. Regrowth uses a cartilaginous supporting structure rather than recreating the original chain of tail vertebrae.

The detailed anatomy, adhesion mechanisms, regrowth biology, and recovery implications belong in our dedicated guide to [skink tail drop and regrowth](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/).

## Are skinks poisonous or dangerous?

**Skinks are not considered venomous.** Their main defensive responses are escape, hiding, tail autotomy in species capable of it, and biting when threatened or restrained.

Bite strength and practical risk vary by species and body size, so this page does not treat all skinks as identical. If you need information about bites, dogs, cats, or common toxicity myths, see [are skinks poisonous or dangerous?](/skinks/are-skinks-poisonous-dangerous/).

## Which skinks are kept as pets?

A number of skink species are kept in captivity, including blue-tongue skinks, fire skinks, crocodile skinks, tree skinks, sandfish skinks, and other species with very different enclosure, diet, humidity, and handling requirements.

Because suitability varies by species, the central entity page should not turn those differences into one universal care standard. Use our [pet skink guide](/pet-skinks/) to compare species and move from general identification into ownership decisions.

## Skinks in gardens and backyards

**Wild skinks commonly use sheltered ground-level habitat such as leaf litter, logs, rocks, garden edges, and low vegetation where those features occur naturally.** Many backyard species feed on insects and other small invertebrates.

People who want to support local skinks can focus on habitat rather than handling them: retain suitable cover, reduce unnecessary pesticide use, and leave safe refuge areas where practical. Regional species and local wildlife rules should always take priority.

For identification, coexistence, and habitat guidance, see our [garden skink guide](/backyard-skinks/garden-skinks/).

## Key facts about skinks

- Skinks are lizards in the family **Scincidae**.
- Smooth, overlapping scales and elongated bodies are common across the family.
- Limb reduction has evolved repeatedly in burrowing skink lineages.
- Skinks occupy terrestrial, fossorial, arboreal, rocky, and semi-aquatic habitats.
- Diet ranges from mainly invertebrate prey to omnivory and herbivory.
- Many species can shed the tail as a defensive response.
- Skink size varies from very small species to large, heavy-bodied forms.

## Frequently asked questions
### Is a skink a lizard?

Yes. A skink is a lizard in the family Scincidae.

### Is a skink a snake?

No. Some skinks look snake-like because of body elongation and limb reduction, but they are lizards.

### Do skink tails grow back?

Many skinks can regenerate a replacement tail after autotomy. The replacement is structurally different from the original tail. See the [tail drop and regrowth guide](/skink-biology/tail-drop-regrowth/) for the detailed mechanism.

### Are skinks good pets?

Some skink species are kept successfully in captivity, but suitability and care requirements vary substantially. Compare species in the [pet skink guide](/pet-skinks/) before using any general care advice.
