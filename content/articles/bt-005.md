# Blue-Tongue Skink Cost & Buying Guide: Prices, Breeders and Captive-Bred Checks

**Blue-tongue skink prices vary by species, subspecies, locality, morph, age, breeder, and region, and the setup can cost as much as—or more than—the animal.** A responsible buying decision therefore combines current market price, captive-bred status, health checks, enclosure readiness, and long-term veterinary access.

This guide follows the buying sequence **purchase price → seller evaluation → captive-bred checks → health inspection → setup cost**.

## How much does a blue-tongue skink cost?

**There is no single evergreen price for a blue-tongue skink.** Current U.S. listings sampled in September 2026 show common Indonesian/Irian Jaya and Northern examples around the low hundreds of dollars, while specialty Northern lines can be substantially higher.

A practical **9 September 2026** U.S. asking-price snapshot is:

| Listing type | Sampled asking prices |
| --- | ---: |
| **Irian Jaya / Indonesian examples** | About **$149–$299** in current sampled listings |
| **Standard Northern examples** | About **$199–$500** in current sampled listings |
| **Specialty / selectively bred Northern lines** | About **$399–$745+** in sampled specialty listings |

These are **asking-price examples, not a guaranteed market average**. Inventory, locality, morph, age, and breeder reputation can move prices quickly, so the date of the snapshot should remain visible.

## Why prices vary

Price can change with:

- species and subspecies;
- locality;
- morph or breeding line;
- age and size;
- sex where reliably known;
- captive-bred status;
- breeder reputation;
- documented parentage;
- seasonal supply;
- shipping.

A higher price does not automatically mean better health or more responsible breeding.

## Captive-bred vs. imported or wild-caught

**Where suitable captive-bred animals are available, they are generally the better starting point for a typical pet keeper.** The RSPCA explicitly advises obtaining a blue-tongue skink bred in captivity in the keeper’s country or considering a rescue.

Ask the seller to state whether the animal is:

- captive-bred (CB);
- captive-hatched (CH);
- imported;
- farmed;
- wild-caught / field-collected.

Do not treat those terms as interchangeable.

## Questions to ask a breeder or seller

Before paying, ask:

1. What is the exact species, subspecies, or locality?
2. What is the scientific name?
3. Was the animal captive-bred, captive-hatched, farmed, imported, or wild-caught?
4. What is the approximate birth date or age?
5. What is it currently eating?
6. How often is it feeding?
7. What temperature, humidity, substrate, and UVB conditions is it being kept under?
8. Has it had any known health problems or veterinary treatment?
9. Can you provide recent photos or video of the actual animal?
10. What are the live-arrival, health, and return policies?

Parentage and hatch records are useful where available, but a seller should not be declared unethical solely because one specific document is unavailable.

## How to inspect a blue-tongue skink before buying

**A visual check can identify obvious concerns, but it cannot diagnose disease.**

Look for:

### Eyes and face

Check for obvious swelling, heavy discharge, crusting, or injury.

### Jaw and mouth

Look for marked deformity, swelling, or obvious injury. Do not force the mouth open for a casual sales inspection.

### Breathing

Repeated open-mouth breathing, wheezing, respiratory effort, or bubbles around the nostrils are warning signs that deserve veterinary assessment.

### Skin and shedding

Look for wounds, burns, severe retained shed, visible parasites, swelling, or abnormal areas of skin.

### Body condition

The animal should not appear markedly emaciated or unable to support itself normally. Tail thickness alone should not be used as a diagnosis of nutritional status.

### Movement and behavior

Observe whether the skink moves normally for the species and situation. A shy animal at an expo is not automatically unhealthy, and a calm animal is not automatically healthy.

## Red flags

Be cautious when:

- the seller cannot identify the species or locality;
- origin changes between conversations;
- “captive-bred” is claimed but the seller cannot explain the animal’s history;
- the seller cannot tell you what the animal is eating;
- recent photos or video of the actual animal are unavailable for a remote sale;
- visible health problems are dismissed without explanation;
- the seller pressures you to pay immediately;
- shipping terms are unclear;
- you are being encouraged to buy before the enclosure is ready.

## Setup cost

**The enclosure and husbandry equipment should be purchased before the animal.** The total starting budget varies with enclosure material, climate, and equipment quality, but it commonly includes:

- a minimum 120 × 60 × 60 cm enclosure;
- heating;
- a thermostat;
- UVB lighting;
- thermometers;
- a hygrometer;
- substrate;
- hides and furnishings;
- water and food dishes;
- initial food and supplements.

Use the [blue-tongue skink enclosure guide](/pet-skinks/blue-tongue-skink/enclosure/) and [skink supplies checklist](/skink-care/supplies-checklist/) to build the equipment budget.

## Veterinary planning

**Identify a reptile-experienced veterinarian before buying.** Veterinary prices vary too much by region and diagnostic need for one evergreen exam fee to be reliable.

An imported animal, an animal with incomplete history, or any skink showing concerning signs may need veterinary examination and diagnostic testing.

Do not start routine deworming or medication simply because the animal is new.

## Buying checklist

Before purchase, confirm:

- [ ] I know the exact taxon/locality.
- [ ] I know whether it is captive-bred, imported, farmed, or wild-caught.
- [ ] I have seen the actual animal recently.
- [ ] I know what it is eating.
- [ ] I understand the seller’s policies.
- [ ] I have checked for obvious health concerns.
- [ ] My enclosure has been running and tested for at least one week.
- [ ] I can afford ongoing food, electricity, replacement equipment, and veterinary care.
- [ ] I have access to a reptile-experienced veterinarian.

## Frequently asked questions
### How much does a Northern blue-tongue skink cost?

In the 9 September 2026 sample, standard Northern listings ranged from roughly $199 to $500, while selectively bred/specialty examples extended to about $745 and higher.

### Should I buy captive-bred?

Where responsibly produced captive-bred stock is available, it is generally preferable for a typical pet buyer because provenance and captive history are easier to establish.

### How do I know whether an animal is truly captive-bred?

Ask for a clear origin history, approximate birth date, breeder information, and feeding/husbandry history. If the explanation is inconsistent, treat the origin as uncertain.

### Should I buy the animal before the enclosure?

No. Set up and test the enclosure first.
