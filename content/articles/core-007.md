# Skink Lifespan, Size & Growth: How Big and How Long Do Skinks Live?

**Skink size and lifespan vary widely by species.** Some skinks are only a few inches long as adults, while large-bodied species can exceed two feet in total length. Captive lifespan also differs substantially: cited sources document roughly 8–12 years for red-eyed crocodile skinks, 10–15 years for fire and sandfish skinks, and 15–20 years for Solomon Island prehensile-tailed skinks.

This page compares **life history → size → growth → maturity → lifespan** across skinks. If you are choosing a pet species, use these comparisons alongside the [pet skink selection guide](/pet-skinks/), because adult size and expected lifespan directly affect enclosure space, long-term cost, and ownership commitment.

## How long do skinks live?

**There is no single lifespan for “a skink.”** Lifespan depends on species, genetics, husbandry, disease, injury, and whether the figure refers to captive or wild animals.

Published lifespan figures include:

| Species | Captive lifespan reported in cited sources |
| --- | --- |
| **Red-eyed crocodile skink (*Tribolonotus gracilis*)** | About **8–12 years** |
| **Fire skink (*Mochlus fernandi*)** | About **10–15 years** |
| **Sandfish skink (*Scincus scincus*)** | About **10–15 years or more** |
| **Solomon Island prehensile-tailed skink (*Corucia zebrata*)** | About **15–20 years** |

These figures should be read as **species-specific expectations**, not as a universal average for Scincidae.

For pet planning, the important question is not “How long do skinks live?” but **“How long can this exact species live in captivity?”**

## Captive lifespan vs. wild lifespan

**Captive and wild lifespan figures should not be treated as interchangeable.** Wild animals face predation, weather, food shortages, parasites, injury, and other pressures that are very different from a managed captive environment.

Captive animals may avoid some of those risks, but longevity still depends on appropriate:

- temperature and humidity;
- diet and supplementation;
- enclosure design;
- body-condition management;
- veterinary care;
- safe handling;
- disease prevention.

A care error can shorten lifespan even in an otherwise long-lived species. For general husbandry, see the [skink care guide](/skink-care/).

## How big do skinks get?

**Adult skink size ranges from very small ground-dwelling species to large, heavy-bodied skinks such as *Tiliqua* and *Corucia*.**

Published evidence illustrates that range clearly:

| Species | Size measure from published evidence | What it shows |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| **Little brown skink (*Scincella lateralis*)** | **3–5 in total length** | A very small ground skink |
| **Common five-lined skink (*Plestiodon fasciatus*)** | **67.7 mm average SVL** | Small-to-medium body size |
| **Red-eyed crocodile skink (*Tribolonotus gracilis*)** | **96.9 mm average SVL** | Compact, medium-bodied skink |
| **Fire skink (*Mochlus fernandi*)** | **8–12 in total length**; **124.8 mm average SVL** in the comparison dataset | Medium terrestrial skink |
| **Blue-tongue skink (*Tiliqua scincoides*)** | **262.1 mm average SVL** in the comparison dataset | Large-bodied skink |
| **Solomon Island prehensile-tailed skink (*Corucia zebrata*)** | **275 mm average SVL** in the comparison dataset | One of the largest-bodied skinks represented in the dataset |

The measurements are not all directly interchangeable because some sources report **total length** while research datasets often use **snout-vent length (SVL)**.

## What is snout-vent length?

**Snout-vent length, or SVL, measures the body from the tip of the snout to the vent and excludes the tail.** It is widely useful when comparing lizard body size because tail length can vary greatly between species and can also change after tail loss.

Total length includes:

**body length + tail length**

That makes total length useful for describing what an animal looks like at full size, but less reliable for scientific comparisons when:

- one animal has lost part of its tail;
- the tail has regenerated;
- two species naturally have very different tail proportions.

For this reason, a 10-inch skink is not necessarily “bigger-bodied” than another 10-inch skink if much more of its length is tail.

## What does “full-grown skink” mean?

**A full-grown skink is an animal that has reached or is close to its normal adult body size for its species.** That does not necessarily mean growth stops abruptly on a specific birthday, and it does not mean every individual reaches exactly the same size.

Adult size is influenced by:

- species;
- sex in species with sexual size differences;
- genetics;
- juvenile nutrition;
- health;
- developmental history.

“Full grown” and “sexually mature” are also not identical concepts. An animal can become reproductively mature before every aspect of adult body mass and proportion has stabilized.

Species-specific profiles are the correct place to check expected adult size and maturity.

## How fast do skinks grow?

**Growth rate is species- and life-stage-specific, so there is no reliable universal rule such as “all skinks double in size within a few months.”**

Juveniles generally undergo more visible growth than established adults, but the rate depends on:

- species;
- birth or hatch size;
- diet;
- temperature;
- health;
- reproductive strategy;
- individual genetics.

Avoid using a feeding schedule from one skink species simply because you want another species to grow faster. Growth should be supported by correct husbandry, not accelerated artificially.

For feeding principles, see [what skinks eat](/skink-care/diet/).

## How to track a pet skink’s growth

**Growth tracking is most useful as a trend over time rather than as a single measurement.**

For a pet skink, useful records can include:

- body weight;
- SVL where it can be measured without excessive stress;
- total length where tail condition is recorded;
- body-condition photographs taken from a consistent angle;
- appetite;
- shedding history;
- date and life stage.

A small digital scale can be useful for monitoring weight trends in species that can be weighed safely.

### Why weight alone is not enough

Weight can change because of:

- recent feeding;
- hydration;
- fecal material;
- reproductive status;
- fat gain or loss;
- illness.

For that reason, one isolated weight should not be treated as a complete health assessment. Look for a **trend** and interpret it alongside body condition and behavior.

If weight declines unexpectedly or growth appears abnormal, review husbandry and seek reptile-veterinary advice where appropriate.

## Which skinks are the biggest?

**Large-bodied skinks include the Solomon Island prehensile-tailed skink and several members of the blue-tongue genus *Tiliqua*.**

Comparative data include:

- *Corucia zebrata* averages about **275 mm SVL**;
- *Tiliqua scincoides* averages about **262 mm SVL**;
- *Tiliqua rugosa* is also represented as a large-bodied skink at roughly **281 mm SVL**.

These figures describe body length without the tail. Total length can be much greater.

If you are considering a large skink as a pet, size affects more than appearance. A larger-bodied species may require:

- more enclosure space;
- larger hides and furnishings;
- more substantial heating and lighting coverage;
- higher food volume;
- greater transport and veterinary handling capacity.

For species selection rather than measurement, return to the [pet skink ownership guide](/pet-skinks/).

## Small skinks vs. large skinks

**Body size does not by itself determine whether a skink is easier to keep.**

A small species may still require:

- specialized humidity;
- deep substrate;
- delicate handling;
- live prey;
- escape-proof housing.

A large species may be easier to observe but require substantially more floor or vertical space.

Size is therefore one ownership variable, not a complete measure of care difficulty.

## Does a bigger skink live longer?

**Do not assume that larger skinks automatically live longer.** Some large species are long-lived, but body size alone is not enough to predict lifespan across the whole family.

Lifespan comparisons should be based on evidence for the species itself because longevity also reflects:

- evolutionary history;
- reproductive strategy;
- metabolism;
- disease risk;
- husbandry;
- captive breeding history.

The safest comparison is species-to-species, not a universal “bigger equals older” rule.

## Lifespan and size as ownership commitments

Before buying a pet skink, ask:

1. **How large will this species be as an adult?**
2. **How much usable enclosure space will that adult require?**
3. **How many years could I realistically be responsible for it?**
4. **Will I still be able to provide housing, food, electricity, and veterinary care later?**
5. **Can I transport and safely handle an adult of this size if medical care is needed?**

A small juvenile can make the future commitment easy to underestimate.

## Frequently asked questions
### What is the average lifespan of a pet skink?

There is no meaningful single average for all pet skinks. Cited sources document roughly 8–12 years for red-eyed crocodile skinks, 10–15 years for fire and sandfish skinks, and 15–20 years for Solomon Island prehensile-tailed skinks.

### How big is a full-grown skink?

It depends on the species. Little brown skinks are only about 3–5 inches in total length, while large-bodied *Tiliqua* and *Corucia* species are many times larger.

### What is the biggest skink?

The Solomon Island prehensile-tailed skink (*Corucia zebrata*) is among the largest living skinks and is represented in the comparative dataset at about 275 mm average SVL. Large *Tiliqua* species are also among the biggest-bodied skinks.

### Do skinks keep growing after maturity?

Growth does not switch off at one universal age across Scincidae. The rate slows as animals approach adult size, and the exact growth pattern depends on species and individual biology.

### How can I tell if my skink is growing normally?

Track weight and body condition over time and compare them with species-specific expectations. Unexpected weight loss, poor body condition, or abnormal development should prompt a review of husbandry and veterinary advice where appropriate.
